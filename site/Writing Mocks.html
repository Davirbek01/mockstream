1.2
Letter to Neighborhood Office (~185 words)
185 words

Answer

Dear Sir or Madam,

Thank you for informing residents about the new safety measures. I genuinely welcome the decision to install cameras and to offer evening safety workshops, because several intersections feel unsafe after dark and many older neighbours have mentioned this. At the same time, I believe security needs to be increased in a way that is proportionate and clearly respectful of people's privacy.

First, it would be sensible to prioritise brighter, energy-efficient street lighting and periodic patrols by trained community volunteers; both actions deter opportunistic crime but do not record personal data. Second, a simple mobile or web-based reporting tool, where residents can submit concerns anonymously, would give quieter or elderly neighbours a voice. Third, teenagers should be involved as partners, not treated as potential offenders. The local school or youth council could help co-design campaign materials, run peer-to-peer safety talks, and even manage the programme's social-media updates so the message actually reaches younger residents.

If safety is built transparently and with the community, it will last longer, cost less, and feel fairer to everyone.

Yours faithfully,
Mr Khasanov

Blog Post (~230 words)
230 words

Answer

If your phone keeps telling you that you've spent four or five hours online, but you don't want to delete Instagram, Telegram or games because you genuinely need them, the answer is smarter habits, not digital exile. The aim is to make the apps work on your terms.

Start with friction. Move the most distracting apps off the first screen, or put them in one folder so that opening them takes two taps. That tiny delay gives your brain time to ask, "Do I really need this now?" Next, use built-in tools such as Screen Time or Digital Wellbeing to set daily limits. Don't block the apps - just make them slightly annoying after, say, 40 minutes.

Another powerful trick is replacement. If you unlock your phone out of boredom, decide in advance what the "good" option is: an audiobook, a language app, or a to-do list. You still use your phone, but your attention goes somewhere useful. Finally, change the context: no-phone meals, no-phone bedroom, and a real alarm clock. When the phone is not always within arm's reach, you scroll less without even noticing.

You keep every app, but you stop letting every app keep you.
